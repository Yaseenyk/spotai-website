"How many sets and reps should I do?" is one of the first questions new gym-goers ask a trainer. The answer is less strict than the "3 sets of 12 for everything" plan many people get on day one, but it does have a few rules.

## Reps: a wide range works

For years, 8 to 12 reps was called the "hypertrophy range". Research now shows muscles can grow across a much wider range, from heavy sets of about 5 reps to light sets of 30, as long as the sets are taken close to failure (Schoenfeld et al., 2017).

In practice, most of your sets should sit in the middle:

| Rep range | Use it for | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3–5 | Strength on big lifts | Builds some muscle, more tiring per set |
| **6–15** | **Most muscle-building work** | Best balance of load, effort and safety |
| 15–30 | Small muscles, machines, finishers | Works if taken close to failure; burns more |

A simple setup: **6 to 10 reps** for big lifts like squats, presses and rows, and **10 to 15 reps** for smaller exercises like curls, lateral raises and leg extensions.

## Effort: how hard each set should be

The number of reps only matters if the set is hard. Ten easy reps with a weight you could lift 25 times does very little.

Use **reps in reserve (RIR)**: how many more good reps you could have done when you stopped.

- **RIR 3:** you could have done three more. Fine for warm-ups and learning form.
- **RIR 1–2:** the last reps slow down and feel hard. **Aim for this on most working sets.**
- **RIR 0:** you could not do another rep. Use sparingly, mainly on machines or the last set.

Beginners usually underestimate how many reps they have left. Once in a while, on a safe exercise like a machine press, do a set to failure to learn what it feels like.

## Sets: count them per week, per muscle

A large review found that doing 10 or more hard sets per muscle group per week led to more muscle growth than doing fewer than five (Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger, 2017). Volume is best counted across the week, not per session.

| Experience | Hard sets per muscle per week | Sets per exercise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First 3 months | 6–8 | 2–3 |
| 3–12 months | 8–12 | 3 |
| 1 year + | 10–20 | 3–4 |

Start at the low end. If you are recovering well and progressing, add a set or two per muscle every few weeks. If you are always sore and your lifts are going down, you are doing too much. See also [how many rest days you need](/guides/rest-days-how-many-per-week/).

## Rest between sets

Short rest feels productive, but longer rest lets you do more reps on the next set. A study comparing one minute with three minutes of rest found more muscle growth with the longer rest (Schoenfeld et al., 2016).

- **Big lifts** (squat, bench press, rows, deadlift): 2 to 3 minutes.
- **Smaller exercises** (curls, raises, extensions): 1 to 2 minutes.

## A sample week: 3 days, full body

Here is how the numbers fit into a beginner week. Each day trains the whole body.

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Main muscles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goblet squat or leg press | 3 × 8–10 | Quads, glutes |
| Dumbbell bench press | 3 × 8–10 | Chest, triceps |
| Lat pulldown or seated row | 3 × 8–12 | Back, biceps |
| Romanian deadlift (dumbbells) | 2 × 10 | Hamstrings, glutes |
| Dumbbell shoulder press | 2 × 10 | Shoulders |
| Plank | 2 × 30 seconds | Core |

Done three times a week, that gives chest, back and quads about 9 sets each per week, right in the beginner range. For a full plan, see our [beginner gym workout plan](/guides/gym-workout-plan-for-beginners/).

## In a busy gym

At 7 pm, the bench and squat rack can have a queue. Keep the sets and reps, and swap the exercise:

- Bench taken: dumbbell press on a flat bench or push-ups with feet raised.
- Rack taken: goblet squats or leg press.
- Lat pulldown taken: one-arm dumbbell rows.

The muscle does not know which machine you used. It knows how many hard sets it did.

## Make it add up over time

Sets and reps are only the start. To keep growing, you need to beat your numbers slowly over weeks: one more rep, a little more weight. That is [progressive overload](/guides/progressive-overload-explained/). Write your sets down after each exercise, or log them in SpotAI, which keeps your history and a view of this week's training.

If an exercise causes joint pain rather than muscle effort, stop and get it checked by a doctor or physiotherapist.
