Biryani is the hardest common Indian food to count. It is rice, meat, fat and spices cooked together, in sizes from a small box to a family handi, and no two kitchens use the same oil. A page that gives "chicken biryani: 290 calories" with no portion or source is guessing. What can be done honestly is to show the ranges from the data that exists, state the weights, and let you choose where your own plate falls.

## What the data says, per 100 g

There are two kinds of source here, and they disagree.

| Chicken biryani, per 100 g | Calories | Protein | Fat | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Survey recipe | 104 kcal | 7.15 g | 2.38 g | USDA FNDDS, "Biryani with chicken" |
| Packaged product A | 163 kcal | 6.01 g | not checked | USDA Branded Foods, label (FDC 1895953) |
| Packaged product B | 140 kcal | 5.49 g | not checked | USDA Branded Foods, label (FDC 2076755) |

USDA's survey database (FNDDS 2021 to 2023) estimates what Americans eat, using a standard recipe. The two packaged products are ready meals sold in the US, with the label values entered by the maker. The ICMR-NIN Indian Food Composition Tables (IFCT 2017) cover raw foods and do not have biryani at all. So there is no official Indian composition for the finished dish, and we are showing the range between a low and a high source rather than choosing one.

## Chicken biryani by portion

Using the survey figure at the low end and packaged product A at the high end:

| Portion | Survey recipe (104 kcal per 100 g) | Packaged product A (163 kcal per 100 g) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 g | 104 kcal | 163 kcal |
| 1 cup, 196 g (USDA survey portion) | about 204 kcal, 14.0 g protein | about 320 kcal, 11.8 g protein |
| 250 g | about 260 kcal | about 408 kcal |
| 245 g (USDA "quantity not specified") | about 255 kcal, 17.5 g protein | about 399 kcal, 14.7 g protein |
| 500 g | about 520 kcal | about 815 kcal |
| 1 kg | about 1,040 kcal | about 1,630 kcal |

Packaged product A comes in a 283 g pack at 163 kcal per 100 g, so the whole pack is about 461 kcal and 17 g of protein. Product B, a 328 g quarter-pack portion at 140 kcal per 100 g, is about 459 kcal and 18 g of protein. These are the only whole-meal numbers in the table that come from an actual product, and they cluster near 460 kcal.

So "1 plate of chicken biryani" is, on this data, somewhere between about 255 and 460 kcal, with a restaurant plate of unknown weight and oil probably at or above the top of that range. We cannot give a better figure than that without a measured plate.

## Why biryani varies so much

Four things drive it, and you can think about each:

- **Rice to chicken ratio.** Cooked rice is about 130 kcal per 100 g (USDA); see [calories in rice](/guides/calories-in-rice/). More rice, more calories per plate, and less protein per 100 g.
- **Oil and ghee.** USDA lists vegetable oil at 884 kcal per 100 g and ghee (butter oil) at 876 kcal per 100 g. A tablespoon of ghee is about 112 kcal on its own. The survey recipe has only 2.38 g of fat per 100 g, which is only about 6 g of fat (a little over a teaspoon) in a 245 g portion. A restaurant dum biryani may use far more.
- **Cut of chicken and the bone.** A portion with bone has less edible meat for its weight.
- **Fried onions, nuts and dried fruit.** These add calories that are not in a plain recipe.

Mutton, called "meat" in the survey data, is higher: 145 kcal and 8.5 g of protein per 100 g, so a 245 g portion is about 355 kcal. Vegetable biryani is listed at 109 kcal and 1.93 g of protein per 100 g, about 234 kcal for a 215 g portion. All of these are from the same survey, so the order between them is more trustworthy than any single figure.

## What goes with biryani

Raita is the usual side. USDA lists plain whole-milk yogurt at 61 kcal and 3.47 g of protein per 100 g. Half a cup (about 122 g) is about 75 kcal and 4.2 g of protein, though Indian curd and raita (with cucumber, onion and spices) are not identical. Salan, mirchi ka salan or a boiled egg add their own. Count each part rather than hoping the plate is "one biryani".

## How to count a biryani you cook at home

At home you can do what no table can: add up the pot.

1. Weigh the **raw rice**. 100 g of raw milled rice is about 356 kcal (IFCT 2017).
2. Add the chicken. USDA and IFCT list chicken at different values by cut; use a figure for the cut you buy and note whether it is raw and boneless.
3. Add every spoon of oil and ghee, and the fried onion.
4. Divide by the number of servings and, better, weigh the finished pot and divide by the weight of your plate.

The steps are in [how to count calories in Indian home food](/guides/how-to-count-calories-in-indian-home-food/). For protein, [highest protein foods in India](/guides/highest-protein-foods-in-india/) lists chicken and other sources per 100 g.

## What to take from this

- Chicken biryani on the data we can source is about 104 to 163 kcal per 100 g.
- A portion is about 255 to 460 kcal on that data; a restaurant plate may be more.
- 500 g is about 520 to 815 kcal and 1 kg about 1,040 to 1,630 kcal.
- Protein is about 14 to 18 g in those portions.
- The oil is the unknown. Ask how it was made if you can.

These are estimates from a survey recipe and two product labels, not measurements of any dish you will eat. This page is food data, not diet advice.
