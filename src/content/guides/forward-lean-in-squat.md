Every squat has some forward lean. To keep your balance, your hips go back as your knees bend, so your torso tilts forward. The question is not whether you lean, but how much, and whether your back angle stays steady through the rep.

## How much lean is normal?

Film a set from the side and look for these:

**Normal**

- Your torso tilts forward but stays roughly the same angle from the bottom to the top.
- Your hips and shoulders rise together on the way up.
- The weight stays over the middle of your foot.

**Too much**

- Your chest drops sharply in the bottom half.
- On the way up, your **hips rise first** and your shoulders follow.
- Your heels lift, or the bar or dumbbell drifts in front of your toes.
- You feel the squat mostly in your lower back.

Body shape matters. If your thighs are long compared with your torso, you will lean more than a friend with short thighs, even with good form. That is not a fault to fix.

## Common causes and what to do

| Cause | Quick check | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Too much weight | Lean gets worse in the last reps or heavier sets | Drop 10–20% and rebuild |
| Knees held back | You were told "knees must not pass toes" | Let knees travel forward over toes |
| Tight ankles | Heels lift, or the wall test below fails | Ankle mobility, heels raised |
| Weak legs (quads) | Hips shoot up out of the bottom | Goblet squats, leg press, pause squats |
| Weak upper back or core | Chest caves under the bar | Brace before every rep; rows, planks |
| Bar position | Bar sits low on the back | Normal for low-bar; try high-bar or front squat |

### About "knees behind toes"

Many beginners in Indian gyms are still told to keep their knees behind their toes. A study of barbell squats found that blocking the knees from moving forward made people lean their torso much further forward and increased the load on the hips and lower back (Fry et al., 2003). For most people, letting the knees move forward in line with the toes is fine and helps you stay upright. If knee movement causes pain, get it checked by a physiotherapist.

### The ankle wall test

Kneel facing a wall with your front foot about 10 cm away. Keep the heel down and push your knee forward to touch the wall. If you cannot, your ankles may be making you lean. Do this stretch for a minute on each side before every leg day.

## Four drills to stay upright

### 1. Goblet squat

Hold a dumbbell vertically against your chest. The weight in front acts as a counterbalance, so it is hard to fold forward. Do **3 sets of 10**, pausing for a second at the bottom.

### 2. Heel-raised squat

Put small plates (1.25 or 2.5 kg) under your heels, or use a slant board. Your knees can move further forward and your torso stays taller. Use it while you work on ankle mobility. **3 sets of 8–10.**

### 3. Pause squat

With a light weight, hold the bottom for two seconds, brace, then stand up with hips and shoulders rising together. The pause removes bounce and shows you where you lose position. **3 sets of 5–6.**

### 4. Tempo squat

Take three seconds to lower, then stand normally. Slow lowering makes you control your back angle. **3 sets of 6–8.**

Add these to the start of your leg day for three or four weeks, then go back to your normal squat at a lighter weight and build up.

## Brace before every rep

Before you go down:

1. Breathe in through your nose, into your belly and sides.
2. Tighten your stomach as if someone is about to poke it.
3. Keep your chest up and eyes on a spot about two metres ahead on the floor.
4. Squat, stand up, then breathe out at the top.

A loose midsection is one of the most common reasons the chest caves.

## Check it on camera

You cannot see your own back angle in a mirror. Put your phone at hip height, side-on, about two metres away. Watch whether your hips and shoulders rise together.
