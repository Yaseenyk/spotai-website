Every dal is described as "high in protein" somewhere, and some pages crown one winner. The truth in the official tables is less dramatic. The five common dals are close, one is a little ahead, and one whole food that people sometimes call a dal, soybean, is far ahead. This guide ranks them on the ICMR-NIN Indian Food Composition Tables (IFCT 2017) and explains why you should not buy dal by this number alone. For how to turn it into a katori, see [protein in dal per 100 g](/guides/protein-in-dal-per-100g/).

## The ranking: split dals, raw, per 100 g

IFCT 2017 lists each dal as raw, dehusked and split. Energy is in kJ and we converted at 4.184 kJ per kcal. The "per 100 kcal" column divides protein by energy.

| Rank | Dal (raw) | Protein per 100 g | Calories | Protein per 100 kcal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Masoor dal (lentil) | 24.35 g | about 322 kcal | about 7.5 g |
| 2 | Moong dal (green gram) | 23.88 g | about 326 kcal | about 7.3 g |
| 3 | Urad dal (black gram) | 23.06 g | about 324 kcal | about 7.1 g |
| 4 | Toor dal (red gram) | 21.70 g | about 331 kcal | about 6.6 g |
| 5 | Chana dal (Bengal gram) | 21.55 g | about 329 kcal | about 6.5 g |

Masoor dal is first, and chana dal is last, by 2.8 g per 100 g. IFCT gives each value with a spread across regions. For masoor the spread is about plus or minus 1.1 g and for chana dal about 1.5 g, so the top and the bottom of the table overlap in what the samples show. We read this as: the dals are close to equal, and masoor, moong and urad are a little higher than toor and chana.

## What a katori changes

The difference becomes small in a real portion. In a katori of home dal made from 30 g of raw dal (our assumption), masoor gives about 7.3 g of protein, moong 7.2 g, urad 6.9 g, toor 6.5 g and chana dal 6.5 g. The gap between first and last is under 1 g. A bigger katori or a thicker dal changes the protein more than the type does: a katori made from 40 g of dal gives 8.6 to 9.7 g across the five. If you want more protein from dal, use more dal, not a different one.

## Other pulses and soybean for comparison

| Raw, per 100 g | Protein | Calories | Protein per 100 kcal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Soybean, brown | 35.58 g | about 381 kcal | about 9.3 g |
| Moong, whole | 22.53 g | about 294 kcal | about 7.7 g |
| Masoor, whole (brown lentil) | 22.49 g | about 299 kcal | about 7.5 g |
| Urad, whole | 21.97 g | about 291 kcal | about 7.5 g |
| Horse gram, whole | 21.73 g | about 330 kcal | about 6.6 g |
| Cowpea, brown | 20.36 g | about 320 kcal | about 6.4 g |
| Rajma, red | 19.91 g | about 299 kcal | about 6.7 g |
| Chana, whole (Bengal gram) | 18.77 g | about 287 kcal | about 6.5 g |

Whole pulses have less protein per 100 g than the matching split dal (IFCT also lists more fibre in them). They also come with fewer calories per 100 g, so their protein per calorie is about the same as the split dal or higher. Soybean stands out at 35.58 g of protein per 100 g and 19.82 g of fat. Dried soya chunks, as in the [highest protein foods in India](/guides/highest-protein-foods-in-india/), are a processed form and not the same as raw soybean.

## Cooked values

IFCT only has raw foods. USDA lists boiled pulses with no salt or fat per 100 g: lentils 9.02 g of protein, chickpeas 8.86 g, cowpeas 7.73 g, mung beans 7.02 g and pigeon peas (toor) 6.76 g. The ordering there is not the same as the raw ranking, since each pulse takes up a different amount of water and these are different samples. This is one more reason not to treat the ranking as exact. For home dal, count the dry dal that went into the pot, not the cooked weight.

## Does it matter which dal you pick?

For protein alone, only a little. Reasons to pick one dal over another, from the same table:

- **Fat.** Chana dal has 5.31 g of fat per 100 g raw. Masoor has 0.75 g. Per katori made from 30 g, that is about 1.6 g against 0.2 g, which is small, but it is why chana dal is 329 kcal against 322 for masoor.
- **Taste, cooking time and digestion.** The tables say nothing about these, and you know what suits you.
- **Price and what is on the shelf.** Not covered here.
- **Variety.** Eating several dals over a week is a simple way to avoid relying on one number.

If your goal is more protein in the day, the bigger levers are portion size and other foods at the meal. A 100 g difference in dal weight beats the difference between masoor and toor. See [how much protein per day for the gym](/guides/how-much-protein-per-day-for-gym/) for targets and [eggs vs paneer](/guides/eggs-vs-paneer-protein/) for two other everyday sources. Roti and rice add a few grams each; see [calories in roti and chapati](/guides/calories-in-roti-chapati/).

IFCT 2017 is a table of average values from samples. Individual bags of dal differ with variety, region and storage. This page is food data, not diet advice. If you have kidney disease or another medical condition, ask a doctor how much protein is right for you.
