Searches for "protein in dal" return one tidy number, and it is nearly always for raw dal or for boiled lentils in a US database. Neither is what is in your katori. This guide shows all three versions, raw, plain boiled and home dal, and how to work out protein for the dal you cook. It does not rank the dals in detail; the numbers are close, as the tables show.

## Protein in dal per 100 g, raw

The ICMR-NIN Indian Food Composition Tables (IFCT 2017) list dal as dehusked split pulses, raw. Energy is in kilojoules, converted here at 4.184 kJ per kcal.

| Dal (raw, dry), per 100 g | Protein | Calories | Fat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Masoor dal (lentil) | 24.35 g | about 322 kcal | 0.75 g |
| Moong dal (green gram) | 23.88 g | about 326 kcal | 1.35 g |
| Urad dal (black gram) | 23.06 g | about 324 kcal | 1.69 g |
| Toor dal (red gram) | 21.70 g | about 331 kcal | 1.56 g |
| Chana dal (Bengal gram) | 21.55 g | about 329 kcal | 5.31 g |

IFCT reports a spread across regions for each value, and the five dals are within about 3 g of each other. In real life they are close to equal. Chana dal has the most fat.

## Protein in dal per 100 g, cooked

IFCT 2017 gives only raw foods, so for cooked pulses we use USDA FoodData Central (boiled, no salt, no fat):

| Boiled, per 100 g | Protein | Calories | A cup weighs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lentils (masoor) | 9.02 g | 116 kcal | 198 g |
| Chickpeas (chana) | 8.86 g | 164 kcal | 164 g |
| Cowpeas | 7.73 g | 116 kcal | 171 g |
| Mung beans (moong) | 7.02 g | 105 kcal | 202 g |
| Pigeon peas (toor) | 6.76 g | 121 kcal | 168 g |

These are whole boiled legumes drained of water, thicker than most home dal. USDA's survey database also has a recipe called simply "Dal" at 8.6 g of protein and 145 kcal per 100 g, which includes added fat; it is a thick American-survey version, not a thin tadka dal.

The point of the cooked table is the gap between raw and cooked. Raw dal at about 22 to 24 g per 100 g becomes about 7 to 9 g per 100 g once it has absorbed water. The protein did not go anywhere; it is spread across more weight. A home dal with a lot of extra water in it will be lower again per 100 g.

## Protein in one katori or bowl of dal

This is the number that matters, and it depends on how much raw dal went into the pot. A reliable way is to count the raw dal and divide by the number of katoris. For example, 150 g of raw dal cooked for five katoris is 30 g per katori.

The table uses the raw weights in IFCT. 20 g matches the NIN Dietary Guidelines manual, whose sample meal plans pair about 20 g of raw pulses with half a cup of dhal (1 cup being 200 ml). 30 g is our assumption for a typical home katori. 40 g is a thick katori.

| Raw dal in the katori | Masoor | Moong | Urad | Toor | Chana |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 20 g | 4.9 g | 4.8 g | 4.6 g | 4.3 g | 4.3 g |
| 30 g | 7.3 g | 7.2 g | 6.9 g | 6.5 g | 6.5 g |
| 40 g | 9.7 g | 9.6 g | 9.2 g | 8.7 g | 8.6 g |

The calories in a katori from 30 g of raw dal are about 97 to 99 kcal before the tadka. The tadka is separate: USDA gives vegetable oil at about 884 kcal per 100 g and ghee (butter oil) at 876 kcal per 100 g, so a teaspoon of either is about 37 to 40 kcal. Our [guide to counting calories in home food](/guides/how-to-count-calories-in-indian-home-food/) shows how to divide a pot of dal into katoris.

A "bowl" (we take 250 ml) often holds more than a katori, so a bowl of the same dal could carry about 1.5 times as much protein. We have not measured this; scale by how much dal and how much water you use.

## How much dal for a protein target

If you want a number to aim at, 20 g of protein from dal alone needs about 82 g of raw masoor dal, or about 93 g of raw chana dal, going by IFCT 2017. That is a lot of dal at one meal, so most people spread it. The usual advice is also to count the other foods at the meal: curd, paneer, eggs and the roti or rice beside the dal all add some. A roti gives about 3 g (see [calories in roti and chapati](/guides/calories-in-roti-chapati/)) and a katori of cooked rice about 2.7 g (see [calories in rice](/guides/calories-in-rice/)).

The [highest protein foods in India](/guides/highest-protein-foods-in-india/) lists soya, paneer, eggs and chicken next to dal, and [how much protein per day for the gym](/guides/how-much-protein-per-day-for-gym/) covers daily targets.

## Things that change the number

- **Soaking and cooking time.** These change texture and water, not the amount of protein in the dal you started with.
- **Water in the pot.** Thin dal has the same protein per katori of raw dal, but less per 100 g of finished dish.
- **Tadka and add-ins.** Oil adds calories, not protein. Spinach, tomato or onion add small amounts.
- **Brand and region.** IFCT shows variation between regions. Treat all figures as estimates.

This is information about foods, not diet advice. If you have kidney disease or another medical condition, ask a doctor before changing how much protein you eat.
