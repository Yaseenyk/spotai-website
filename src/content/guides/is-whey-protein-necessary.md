Walk into almost any Indian gym and someone will tell you to "start whey". It can be useful. It is not required. Here is how to decide for yourself.

## What whey protein actually is

Whey is one of the two main proteins in milk. When milk is turned into paneer or cheese, the liquid left behind (the whey) is dried and filtered into powder. A typical scoop lists about 20 to 25 g of protein, depending on the brand.

Your body treats it like other good-quality protein. Whey digests quickly and is rich in leucine, an amino acid linked to muscle building, but over a full day, total protein matters far more than the source.

## How much protein you need

| Who | Daily protein | For a 60 kg person |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adults, basic need (ICMR-NIN) | 0.83 g per kg | about 50 g |
| People who train regularly (ISSN) | 1.4–2.0 g per kg | about 85–120 g |

A 2018 review of 49 studies found that extra protein helped muscle gain from training, but the benefit levelled off at around 1.6 g per kg a day for most people. Beyond that, more protein added little.

## Check your own day first

Before you buy anything, add up a normal day. Here is an example for a 60 kg vegetarian:

| Meal | Food | Protein (approx.) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breakfast | 2 idlis, sambar, 1 cup tea with milk | 8 g |
| Lunch | 2 rotis, 1 katori dal, sabzi, rice | 14 g |
| Evening | Biscuits and tea | 3 g |
| Dinner | Rice, rajma, curd | 15 g |
| **Total** | | **about 40 g** |

That is below even the basic need. But the fix does not have to be powder:

| Add this | Protein added (approx.) |
| --- | --- |
| A second katori of dal at lunch | 7 g |
| 100 g paneer at dinner | 18 g |
| A glass of milk after the gym | 8 g |
| 30 g roasted chana instead of biscuits | 6 g |
| A cup of curd at breakfast | 7 g |

Those five swaps add about 45 g and take the day to roughly 85 g, which is inside the training range. For more ideas, see our [high-protein Indian vegetarian diet guide](/guides/high-protein-indian-vegetarian-diet-for-gym/) and [eggs vs paneer](/guides/eggs-vs-paneer-protein/).

## When whey makes sense

- **You keep falling short** of your target even after adding protein foods.
- **You do not eat much at a time**, or have a small appetite.
- **You are cutting calories** and want protein without many extra calories.
- **Your schedule is tight**: early office shifts, hostels, long commutes where a shaker is easier than a meal.
- **You do not eat eggs or meat** and find large amounts of dal and paneer hard to manage.

## When you can skip it

- You already reach your target from food most days.
- Your budget is tight. Paneer, soya chunks, eggs, curd and chana often cost less per gram of protein than powder. Compare for yourself (see below).
- You are not training regularly yet. Build the gym habit first.

## What to check before you buy

A 2024 study in the journal *Medicine* tested 36 protein supplements sold in India and found that about 70% did not contain the protein amount stated on the label. Some had much less. So choose carefully:

1. **FSSAI licence number** on the pack. Protein supplements are regulated by FSSAI.
2. **Protein per scoop and per 100 g** on the label, not only on the front.
3. **Short ingredient list.** Avoid "proprietary blends" and added herbal mixes you do not need.
4. **Independent lab testing**, if the brand publishes it.
5. **Buy from the brand or an authorised seller.** Fakes are common on unofficial listings.
6. **Cost per gram of protein.** Price ÷ (scoops in the pack × grams of protein per scoop). Do the same sum for paneer or eggs and compare.

## How to use it if you do

- One scoop with water or milk on days you fall short is enough for most people.
- Timing matters less than your daily total. After training is convenient, but any time works.
- Count it as part of your day, not on top of a day that is already enough.

If you have a kidney or liver condition, are pregnant, or take regular medication, speak to your doctor before using protein supplements.
